# Week 32 Teacher Guide

## 1. Header

- **Week:** 32 of 32
- **Unit:** 6, The Future of Computing
- **Theme question:** What did you build, and what does it stand on?
- **Session length:** 2 hours

## 2. Learning objectives

By the end of the session, each student can:

- Demonstrate a working program to an audience in five minutes, live, within time.
- Explain any line of their own code accurately when asked.
- Name honestly what is broken in their own project and what they would do next.
- Ask one substantive question about another student's project.
- Trace their own program down through the layers it depends on, answering the course's opening question about something they built themselves.
- For AP-track students: complete and submit the Create Performance Task through the AP Digital Portfolio.

## 3. Where this sits

The last session. It is a work block, then demos, then one final Mystery Day, then the course ends.

The demos are not a formality. Presenting a program, taking questions on it, and being honest about its limits is the closing assessment of the whole course and it is 15 of the project's 100 points. It is also the only moment all year where every student sees what everyone else built, which for a small mixed-age class matters more than the grade.

The course opened in Week 1 with "how does a button press become something useful?" and answered it at the mid-year milestone in Week 21 by tracing somebody else's software. Today the same question gets asked about software the student wrote. That is the arc, and it is worth naming out loud in the last three minutes rather than letting the session end on a Mystery Day.

For AP-track students, submission is the hard deadline of the day. Everything else on this schedule can flex; the portfolio submission cannot.

## 6. Minute-by-minute class flow

### Segment 1: Setup, submission triage, and last checks (0:00 to 0:15)

1. **Post the schedule and the deadline** on the board, both the College Board date and the hosting school's internal cutoff if there is one.
2. **Triage in the first three minutes.** Ask two questions of the room and act on the answers immediately: whose program does not currently run, and who has not yet uploaded to the portfolio. Those students get you for the next twelve minutes and nobody else does.
3. **Everyone else does the cold-run check** while you triage: fresh terminal, run from the top, all the way through the demo path, once. Anything that breaks gets fixed now, not during the demo.
4. **Connect every presenting machine to the projector once,** in schedule order, ten seconds each. This is worth the two minutes it costs.
5. **Collect the writeups.** Paper or committed, but collected before demos start, because after the demos the room disperses.

### Segment 2: Final polish and AP submission block (0:15 to 0:40), protected

- **Students do:** Last fixes only. State the rule again: nothing new, only repairs and rehearsal. Students who are finished rehearse their five minutes quietly or help someone who is not finished.
- **AP-track students do:** Submit. Walk each one through it individually rather than announcing instructions to the room:
  1. Program code as a PDF, per the current specification.
  2. Video file, within the current length and size limits, with no identifying information and no narration if narration is still prohibited.
  3. Personalized Project Reference, with both required screen captures.
  4. Upload all items to the AP Digital Portfolio through the hosting school's exam-only section.
  5. **Press final submit.** This is a separate action from uploading and it is the step people miss. Have the student show you the confirmation on screen before you walk away.
  6. Complete the attestation honestly.
- **You do:** Finalize the nine-hour log with the time from today. Keep it; you may need to attest to it.
- **Verify all of the above against the current official requirements.** The specifications and the workflow change annually and this guide is not the authority.

### Segment 3: Demo Day, round one (0:40 to 1:10)

1. **Set the audience's job before the first demo,** because an audience with a job listens differently. Hand out the question cards. Every student writes one question for each of three presenters, hands the card to the presenter afterwards, and at least one question per presenter gets asked out loud.
2. **Give the two audience rules.** Questions are about the program, not about the person. "Why did you choose a dictionary there?" is a good question; "why didn't you just use a database?" is a worse one, and asking it kindly is part of the skill.
3. **Run the schedule strictly.** Five minutes of demo, two minutes of questions, one minute to swap machines. Eight minutes per student.
4. **Time it visibly.** Put a timer on the board or hold up a card at four minutes. Running over is not generosity to the presenter, it is time taken from the last student on the list, who is usually the most nervous one.
5. **Ask your prepared code question during the question period,** every time, for every student. Point at the line, ask what it does, and write down the answer on the scoring sheet. Do this evenly across all students so it reads as routine rather than as an accusation.
6. **Score on the sheet as you go.** You will not remember eight demos accurately at the end.

### Segment 4: Stretch (1:10 to 1:15)

### Segment 5: Demo Day, round two (1:15 to 1:45)

- Same format, same rules, same strictness.
- **If you finish early,** do not fill the time with a lecture. Use it for the best part of a demo day, which is students asking each other how they did something. Ask one specific cross-project question yourself to start it: "Two of you solved the same problem in different ways. Say why."
- **If you are running behind,** cut the Mystery Day rather than cutting anyone's demo. The Mystery Day is a nice ending; a student who does not get to present is a bad one.

**Scaling the schedule.** The block above holds roughly eight students at eight minutes each. Adjust as follows and decide before class rather than during:

| Class size | Per student | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| 4 to 6 | 10 min (5 demo, 4 questions, 1 swap) | One block, no round two needed; use the extra time for cross-project discussion |
| 7 to 9 | 8 min (5 demo, 2 questions, 1 swap) | Two blocks as scheduled above |
| 10 to 12 | 6 min (4 demo, 1 question, 1 swap) | Two blocks, cut the Mystery Day, shorten the polish block to 15 minutes |
| 13 or more | Split across two sessions | Demo half in Week 31's second hour and half today |

### Segment 6: Mystery Day (1:45 to 1:57)

Pick one during prep. All three are complete below; run only one.

**Option A: How does Face ID work?**

1. Ask how a phone tells one face from another. Take theories; expect "it takes a photo and compares it."
2. Kill that one. A photo comparison would be defeated by a photograph, and it would fail in different lighting. Ask what else could be measured.
3. Give the actual mechanism in three steps. An infrared projector throws a grid of thousands of invisible dots onto the face. An infrared camera photographs where those dots land, and the distortion of the grid gives a depth map, which is a three-dimensional shape rather than a picture. That shape is fed to a small neural network on the device.
4. Connect it to Week 27: the network turns the face into a list of numbers, an embedding, and unlocking is a check of whether the new list is close enough to the stored one. This is the same "meaning as coordinates" idea, applied to geometry.
5. Connect it to Week 28: the stored numbers live in a separate secure area of the chip, they never leave the device, and they are not a picture, so a leak would not yield a photograph of anyone's face.
6. Close with the tradeoff question. Ask what happens with a twin, or with a very good mask, and note that every biometric system sets a threshold and therefore chooses its own rate of false accepts against false rejects. There is no setting that has neither.

**Option B: How does Netflix stream 4K?**

1. Do the arithmetic first, roughly, on the board. Raw 4K video is about 8 million pixels per frame, three colors each, 24 or more frames a second. That is gigabytes per minute, and no home connection carries it.
2. Ask what has to happen, and let them say compression. Connect straight back to Week 2: lossy compression, the same family as JPEG, throwing away detail people do not notice.
3. Add the trick specific to video, which they will not guess. Most of one frame is identical to the frame before it. Video codecs store a few complete frames and then store only the differences, plus how blocks of pixels moved. That is where most of the saving comes from.
4. Add adaptive bitrate. The film is encoded several times at several qualities and cut into short chunks. The player measures the connection continuously and requests the next chunk at whatever quality it thinks will arrive in time. That is why quality visibly steps down and back up rather than buffering.
5. Connect it to Week 29. The chunks come from a CDN cache near the viewer, often inside their own internet provider's network, not from a distant origin server. Ask why that matters and get to both bandwidth cost and latency.
6. Close with the layer stack, which is the whole course in one answer: compression from Unit 1, the codec running on hardware acceleration from Unit 2, the OS scheduling the decode from Unit 4, TCP and HTTP from Unit 4, the CDN from Unit 6, and the display pipeline from Unit 2.

**Option C: How do multiplayer games stay in sync?**

1. State the problem precisely. Two players are 3,000 kilometers apart, and every message takes tens of milliseconds each way. Ask what the game shows each player during those milliseconds, given that neither can know the other's current position.
2. Give the naive design and let the class break it. Every action goes to the server and the client waits for the answer. It is perfectly consistent and it feels terrible, because pressing a key does nothing for a tenth of a second.
3. Give the real design in two parts. **Client-side prediction:** the client runs the game rules itself and moves your character immediately, assuming the server will agree. **Server reconciliation:** the server is authoritative, and when its answer arrives disagreeing with the prediction, the client corrects. A rubber-band snap is exactly that correction being visible.
4. Add interpolation for other players. Your client draws the other player slightly in the past, between two known positions, because guessing between two known points is far more reliable than extrapolating past the last one.
5. Connect it to Week 29 in one line, and make this the point of the segment: this is the CAP tradeoff in a game. Perfect consistency means unplayable lag; responsiveness means everyone's screen is briefly, slightly wrong. Games chose availability, and the entire discipline of netcode is managing how wrong.
6. Close with why the server is authoritative anyway. If the client were trusted, the client would be modified, and cheating is exactly the untrusted-input problem from Week 28.

### Segment 7: Course close (1:57 to 2:00)

Three minutes, and worth planning rather than improvising.

1. **Ask the opening question one last time,** pointing at the board: how does a button press become something useful? Take one answer from the youngest student in the room. Thirty-two weeks ago nobody could have answered it.
2. **Name what changed, concretely rather than warmly.** They can write a program, read an error, choose a data structure, use a terminal, use version control, ship a web app, explain a network, explain a CPU, explain a neural network, and argue both sides of a hard question about all of it. Say it as a list; the list is the evidence.
3. **Hand out the Week 32 sheet** and point at the "where to go next" section rather than at homework. Note the extra-credit tracks in Section 9 of the curriculum are still open all summer, and that the ambitious ones, the Build the Stack track especially, are genuinely a summer project.
4. **Say the last thing.** Everything they used this year was built by people, most of it is readable, and none of it is magic. That was the whole argument of the course and today they demonstrated their own contribution to it.

## 7. Key scripts and analogies

- **Setting up the audience:** "Your job while someone else presents is not to wait for your turn. It is to find the one question about their program that they will be glad someone asked."
- **On the code question:** "I am going to point at one line in everyone's project and ask what it does. Everyone, every time. It is not a suspicion, it is the check that makes 'I built this' mean something."
- **On going over time:** "Every minute you run over comes out of the last person's demo. Finish on time; it is a professional skill and it is also just courtesy."
- **On the honesty minute:** "Telling us what is broken is the most senior thing you will do today. Junior engineers say it works. Experienced ones say what it does not do yet, and why."
- **On demos that fail live:** "The demo gods take their tribute. Talk us through what should have happened, show the code, and move on. Nobody in this industry has escaped this."
- **Face ID:** "It is not a photograph, it is a shape. Thirty thousand invisible dots, a depth map, and a list of numbers that never leaves the chip."
- **Netflix:** "Most of this frame is the same as the last frame. Once you notice that, video compression is mostly bookkeeping about what moved."
- **Multiplayer:** "Both screens are always slightly wrong. The game's job is to be wrong in ways you do not notice, and to fix it before you do."
- **Closing:** "None of it is magic. All of it was built by people, most of it can be read, and you spent a year proving that to yourselves."

## 8. Differentiation

- **Younger or newer students:** Schedule them second, once the format is established and while the room is still fresh. Let them present sitting down and reading from notes; both are fine and neither costs points. If the five minutes is genuinely too much, agree in advance on a three-minute version and score the demo row against that agreement rather than against the clock. Prepare an easier code question for them, and pick a line you know they wrote, so the check succeeds rather than exposing them.
- **Extensions for advanced or AP-track students:** Have them demonstrate their tests running as part of the five minutes. Ask a harder code question, one about a design decision rather than a line: why this structure and not another. After submission, point them at what comes next: the AP CSA route in Java, which colleges credit more reliably, and the Build the Stack extra-credit track from Section 9, which is a genuine summer project and the most memorable path out of this course.

## 9. Common pitfalls

- **The projector consumes twenty minutes.** Test every machine during prep and again in Segment 1. This is the single most reliable way to lose a demo slot.
- **Demos running long and the last student getting four minutes.** Time visibly and cut at five. The schedule protects the person at the end of it, who is usually the one who needs protecting.
- **The AP submission left to the end.** It has an external deadline and no flexibility. Do it in Segment 2 and watch the confirmation screen with your own eyes.
- **Final submit not pressed.** Uploaded is not submitted. Every year someone finds this out in May.
- **A student's program failing live.** Expect at least one. Have them narrate what should happen, show the code, and continue. Score the demo on the explanation, not on the machine's mood, and say so to the room the first time it happens.
- **The code question skipped for some students.** If it is asked selectively it becomes an accusation. Ask it of everyone, in the same tone, in the same slot.
- **A student who cannot explain their code.** Do not resolve it in front of the room. Score the demo row honestly, move on, and have the conversation privately afterwards.
- **Audience questions turning into criticism.** Set the rule before the first demo and interrupt the first violation, which sets the tone for everything after.
- **The session ending on the Mystery Day.** It is a good segment and it is a bad ending. Protect the last three minutes for the course close.
- **Nothing collected.** Writeups, scoring sheets, and question cards all vanish once the room breaks up. Collect the writeups in Segment 1.

## 11. Assessment

**This session closes the final project, which is 20 percent of the course grade.** Score against the rubric in Section 11 of the Week 31 guide, which students already have. Today supplies the last two rows.

**The demo, 15 points.** Score on the sheet during the presentation, not from memory afterwards. Within it:

- Ran live, or the failure was handled with a clear explanation of what should have happened. A failed demo explained well is worth more than a working demo explained badly.
- Stayed within time.
- Explained one piece of their own code clearly and without reading it aloud verbatim.
- **Answered the code question accurately.** A student who cannot explain their own line does not score above 7 of 15 here, regardless of the program. This is the AI policy's enforcement mechanism and it has been announced since Week 27.
- Was honest in the final minute. "Nothing is broken" scores down.

**The writeup, 15 points,** collected in Segment 1 and marked afterwards. The criterion is the layers paragraph. A writeup that lists features instead of naming what the program stands on does not score above half, however well written, because that paragraph is the capstone.

**Participation** for the session includes the audience question cards. A student who wrote three substantive questions about other people's projects participated; a student who wrote "cool project" did not.

**Course-level closure.** Once the projects are marked, the grade is complete: weekly labs at 40 percent, six unit checkpoints at 20 percent, the two milestone assessments at 15 percent, the final project at 20 percent, and participation at 5 percent, with extra credit added on top and never subtracted. Keep the portfolio of student work, meaning the repositories, the lab artifacts, and the final projects, as the mastery evidence for the credit; Section 2 of the curriculum explains why that portfolio matters for a competency-based credit award.

**AP-track students are not finished.** The end-of-course exam falls after this course ends. Note who is testing, when, and what preparation they still owe themselves.
