# Computer Science: Parent Syllabus

A plain-language guide to the course for parents. It covers what the course is, what it asks of your family, how grading works, and what you need to do before it starts.

## What the course is

This is a 32-week, full-year high school computer science course for our co-op, taught in person, one 2-hour class per week. The whole year is organized around one question: how does a button press become something useful? By June, students can trace what happens from a mouse click all the way through the computer, the operating system, and the internet to the picture on the screen.

Students learn real programming in Python, and they learn it deeply: the course writes code every single week, all year. Around that core, they open up every layer of the machine: they build logic circuits on a breadboard, take apart a real PC, set up a physical network, use the terminal, build a small web app, and finish with a substantial final project of their own design. There are also regular offline logic games (no computer involved) that teach the ideas physically before students meet them on screen.

The class mixes ages from 8th to 11th grade by design. Everyone does the same core work; younger students get extra scaffolding in the few spots where math maturity matters, and older or faster students get optional extra-credit project tracks.

## Weekly time commitment

One 2-hour class per week for 32 weeks. Outside class, expect roughly 30 to 60 minutes of homework most weeks, kept deliberately light and doable by a true beginner. During the final project window in the spring, students who are into their project will happily spend more. Counting class, labs, homework, and the final project, the year adds up to a defensible full high school credit.

No preparation is required before the course. Week 1 starts from zero, including how to use a laptop for students who have only used iPads.

## The year at a glance

| Weeks | Unit | What students do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 5 | Thinking Like a Computer Scientist | First programs in Python; binary; how programmers think |
| 6 to 10 | Inside the Computer | Transistors to CPU; build logic gates; take apart a real PC |
| 11 to 16 | Programming Like a Professional | The programming core: data structures, algorithms, debugging |
| 17 to 21 | Operating Systems and the Internet | The terminal; networking; trace a web page loading end to end |
| 22 to 26 | Building Modern Software | Git, web pages, APIs; build and ship a small web app |
| 27 to 32 | The Future of Computing | AI, cybersecurity, cloud; the final project |

## How grading works

Grading is based on projects and portfolios, not exams. Weekly labs and in-class builds count 40 percent, short unit checkpoints 20 percent, two milestone assessments 15 percent, the final project 20 percent, and participation 5 percent. Labs are graded on a simple scale that rewards working code the student can explain. Extra-credit tracks only add; skipping them never hurts a grade. Students keep a portfolio of their work through the year as evidence of what they built.

## A note on AI tools

For most of the year, students may not use AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot) for coursework. You cannot learn fundamentals if a tool writes them for you, the same way you learn arithmetic before you get a calculator. Late in the year, the course explicitly teaches how to use AI coding tools well, and after that unit they are permitted on regular projects. Please help hold this line at home.

## Accounts and parental consent

A few online accounts are needed, and because these services have age rules, account setup is homework done with a parent, never in class. Written parental consent is collected before any account is created for a minor. Each student needs an email address as a prerequisite (a parent-supervised address is fine), then a Google account for shared documents and a CodeAI account (the free service formerly called Code.org) for concept lessons; a GitHub account is added mid-year for the programming work. Google and GitHub require users to be 13; for younger students, use a parent-managed Google account via Family Link and plan GitHub with the instructor. AI services are never student accounts; the instructor owns and mediates any AI access used in class. A College Board account is only needed if your student opts into the AP exam.

The enrollment agreement also discloses the safety setup on class laptops: content filtering that stays on at home, and a parent-owned monitoring option. Nothing is covert, and the instructor does not collect browsing histories.

## Devices and materials

Class laptops are configured by the instructor (Mac primarily, Windows works equally well) with a standard student account, security enabled, and all course software preinstalled, which is all free. Families need reliable internet at home for homework. There is no required textbook and nothing to buy; a list of optional, enjoyable books that pair with the course is available on request.

## Optional head start

Nothing is required before Week 1, but if your student wants a head start, two things help most: typing practice at typing.com or keybr.com (15 minutes a day makes everything easier), and the free absolute-beginner computer basics videos at training.digitallearn.org. Both are optional.

## The optional AP track, in plain terms

The course covers the material of AP Computer Science Principles, a college-level introductory course from the College Board. Students who want the AP credential can opt in: they work through a free, self-paced online AP curriculum (Project STEM) alongside class, complete an official project in the spring, and sit the AP exam in May at a local school that hosts outside testers. One scheduling note for families of AP students: the College Board requires nine hours of supervised class time for the official project, so from late winter until it is submitted, AP students stay for a supervised extra hour after class each week, with make-up time available by arrangement. Students not taking the AP track never need this hour. The exam fee is roughly $100; verify the current fee, dates, and a hosting school with the instructor early in the fall, since schools set ordering deadlines.

The important part: this is opt-in per student and completely exam-free for everyone else. No student takes an AP exam as a course requirement, no AP work affects the regular course grade, and younger students are never under exam pressure. A student who skips the AP track still earns a full Computer Science I credit and learns everything the course teaches.
