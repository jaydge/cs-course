# Week 32: The Last Sheet

There is no homework. This is the wrap-up: what to hand in, what happens to your work now, and where to go next if you want to keep going. Plan on about 15 minutes to read it and work the hand-in checklist in Section 1, which is the only part with a deadline. If you are taking the AP exam, the last section adds review work that runs between now and exam day rather than this week.

## 1. Hand-in checklist, one last time

- [ ] Repository pushed, with everything in it
- [ ] README saying what it is, what it needs, and the exact command to run it
- [ ] License file
- [ ] One-page writeup handed in
- [ ] Demo done
- [ ] Any AI-assisted code carries its disclosure comment

If you are submitting an AP Create Performance Task:

- [ ] Program code uploaded
- [ ] Video uploaded
- [ ] Personalized Project Reference uploaded
- [ ] **Final submit pressed**, which is a different button from uploading
- [ ] Attestation completed
- [ ] You know where your printed Personalized Project Reference is, because you need it in the exam room

**Until you press final submit, the Create Task rule still holds in full.** No AI at any stage of it: not the code, not the last-minute fix, not the written pieces, not today. It is College Board's rule, you are signing a statement that the work is yours, and nobody here can waive it.

## 2. Keep your work

Do not delete anything. Your repositories are the evidence of what you did this year, and they are worth more than the grade.

Specifically, keep:

- Every program from Week 1 onward. Your Week 2 greeting program is genuinely worth rereading in a year.
- The final project repository, public if you are comfortable with that.
- The writeup. It is the best short description of what you understand, and it is the thing to point at when someone asks what you learned.

If you set up a portfolio site in Unit 5, put the final project on it with a screenshot and two sentences. It takes twenty minutes and it will be useful sooner than you think.

## 3. What you can do now that you could not in September

Read this list. It is longer than you remember agreeing to.

You can write a program with variables, conditionals, loops, functions, lists, dictionaries, and classes. You can read a traceback and find the actual mistake. You can pick a data structure on purpose and say why. You can say what an algorithm costs. You can write a test. You can use a terminal, move around a filesystem, and run things without an IDE. You can use Git and work from a repository. You can build a web page, a Flask app, and something that talks to an API. You know what is inside the computer, from a transistor to a logic gate to a CPU to memory to storage. You know what the operating system does and why. You can trace a request from a browser to DNS to a router to a server and back. You can explain how a neural network learns and why a language model makes things up. You can explain hashing, key exchange, and why the padlock does not mean the site is honest. And you can argue both sides of a hard question about all of it.

That is a real year.

## 4. Where to go next

The extra-credit tracks are in Section 9 of the course plan and they do not expire. Some suggestions depending on what you liked most:

- **You liked building things people can use.** Web development track. Take your Flask app further, add a database, deploy it somewhere real.
- **You liked the hardware weeks.** The Raspberry Pi and Arduino track, or the Build the Stack track: nandgame, then Turing Complete, then nand2tetris. That last one takes you from a single NAND gate to a working computer and a language you wrote yourself. It is the most ambitious path out of this course and it is a genuine summer project.
- **You liked the AI week.** Train a small classifier with scikit-learn, or build a chatbot on top of an API with a parent's involvement, since the accounts are age-restricted.
- **You liked the security week.** picoCTF, at `https://picoctf.org`, now part of Carnegie Mellon's CyLab Security Academy. Legal, deliberately vulnerable, and built for beginners.
- **You liked games.** Pygame. Pong, then Snake, then something of your own.

And one more, which is not on any track: pick a program you use every day and go find out how it works. That habit is the actual thing this course was trying to install.

## 5. Books, if you want them

Not required, not graded, just good.

- *Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and Software*, Charles Petzold. The single best book for someone who just finished this course.
- *But How Do It Know?*, J. Clark Scott. Shorter, and covers the CPU beautifully.
- *The Pragmatic Programmer*, Hunt and Thomas. About how to work, not about a language.

## Extra Credit AP Track

Optional. This section is for students on the AP track and for anyone who finds this stuff interesting, whether for fun or for AP preparation. It is extra credit only: never required, and it is not part of your base grade. Skipping it costs you nothing.

**If you are taking the exam, you are not finished.** The course ends today; the AP exam does not. Here is what is left.

**One record to close out.** Before or at final submission, confirm with the instructor that your logged supervised hours total at least nine. The log is the course's evidence that the College Board time requirement was met, and it closes when you submit.

**What is already done.** The Create Performance Task, submitted through the AP Digital Portfolio. That is 30 percent of your score and it is behind you. It is also the only place the exam assesses Practice 6, Responsible Computing, so that whole strand is now complete.

**What is left.** The end-of-course exam: multiple choice plus two written responses, taken in the Bluebook app. The written responses are about your own Create Task, in AP pseudocode, which is why you print your Personalized Project Reference and take it into the room with you. Know where that printout is.

**Your unit from here.** Only one thing left, and it is review.

- **Project STEM (the AP spine):** the AP Exam Review unit. This is now the whole of your remaining AP path. If the unit numbering on your account does not match, ask your instructor; the numbering is being confirmed.
- **CodeAI, formerly Code.org (free alternative):** finish Unit 9, Create PT Prep, at `https://studio.code.org/courses/csp-2025/units/9`. This is the last of the four slices you have been taking since Week 26, so what is left is the final review and submission lessons. You did the requirements and scoring lessons in Week 27, the written-response lessons in Week 30, and the planning and build lessons in Week 31. After that, use the assessment banks in the units covering your weakest big ideas. For most students that is Unit 5, Data, at `https://studio.code.org/courses/csp-2025/units/5`, and Unit 8, Cybersecurity and Global Impacts, at `https://studio.code.org/courses/csp-2025/units/8`.

**Where to spend your remaining study time.** This course went past exam depth on Big Idea 3, Algorithms and Programming, because programming was the point of it. It covered Big Idea 2, Data, and Big Idea 5, Impact of Computing, more lightly. Those two are 17 to 22 percent and 21 to 26 percent of the multiple-choice exam respectively, which is nearly half of it between them. That is where your review time belongs, not in Big Idea 3 where you are already strong.

Open `ap-track/AP-CSP-Topic-Coverage.md`, find the topics you marked shaky in Week 30, and work those.

**The pseudocode.** Read the official reference sheet again, at `https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-computer-science-principles-exam-reference-sheet.pdf`. You get this on exam day, so the goal is not memorizing it, it is being fluent enough that you never lose a minute translating. `ap-track/AP-Pseudocode-Bridge.md` has the Python-to-pseudocode tables and trace problems. Remember that AP lists are 1-indexed and Python lists are 0-indexed; that single difference costs more exam points than anything else on the sheet.

**Verify your dates.** Exam date, exam location, arrival time, and what you are allowed to bring. Also confirm your registration with the hosting school's AP coordinator if you have not heard from them recently. The fee is roughly $98 to $101 and you use homeschool code 970000 on exam day, but confirm both, since these change year to year and none of this handout is an authority on them. Everything official is at `https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-computer-science-principles`.

**And afterwards.** If you liked this and want the credential colleges credit most reliably, AP Computer Science A is the Java-based exam and is the sensible year-two target. Nothing about it will feel unfamiliar after this year except the syntax.

Good luck.
