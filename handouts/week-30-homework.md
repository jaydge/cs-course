# Week 30 Homework: Who Does This Help, and Who Does It Hurt?

Today was the one class this year with no right answer at the end of it. The homework is mostly writing, and one part of it asks you to argue for something you do not believe. Plan on about 50 minutes for items 1 to 4, with your project on top of that rather than inside it, and the project is now the priority.

## 1. One innovation, both sides

Pick a computing innovation you use regularly. Not one from class; pick your own. Write three short things:

- Two ways it makes something better and two ways it makes something worse, naming who for in each case.
- One effect that is good for one group and bad for another **at the same time**. Name both groups.
- One sentence on whether the harmful effects look like something the designers intended or something nobody saw coming.

## 2. Argue the other side

Pick one of the questions we debated in class. Identify which side you are actually on. Then write four or five sentences making the **strongest possible case for the side you disagree with**.

Rules:

- No "some people think." Write it as though you hold the position.
- No weak version. If your paragraph would make someone on that side wince, you have not done the exercise.
- Do not add a rebuttal at the end. Just the case.

You will be marked on one thing only: would a person who actually holds that position read your paragraph and say "yes, that is my argument"? Whether you agree with it is not part of the grade, and neither is which side you picked.

## 3. Find the bias

A company builds a program to screen job applications. It is trained on ten years of the company's own hiring decisions, and it learns to predict which applicants the company hired. It never sees anyone's name, age, race, or gender; those fields are removed before training.

Answer:

1. Even with those fields removed, how could this system still end up favoring one group over another? Give a specific mechanism, not just "it could be biased."
2. Which of the three sources of bias from class is doing the most work here: the data, the design, or the builders? Defend your pick in two sentences.
3. Name one thing the company could do to find out whether the system is discriminating. Then name the awkward problem with your own suggestion.

## 4. License your project

Open your project repository on GitHub and find out what license it currently has. For most of you the answer is none.

1. Write down what "no license" legally means for someone who finds your code and wants to use it. The answer is not "they can use it."
2. Look at `https://choosealicense.com`, pick between MIT, GPL, and keeping it private, and write two sentences saying which you chose and why.

You will add the license file in Week 31. This week just decide.

## Extra Credit AP Track

Optional. This section is for students on the AP track and for anyone who finds this stuff interesting, whether for fun or for AP preparation. It is extra credit only: never required, and it is not part of your base grade. Skipping it costs you nothing.

**This is the biggest AP week of the whole course.** Big Idea 5, Impact of Computing, is 21 to 26 percent of the multiple-choice exam, and today covered five of its six topics. The sixth was Week 28. If you take one AP week seriously, take this one.

The topics and what the exam wants from each:

- **5.1 Beneficial and Harmful Effects.** Every innovation has both. Harmful effects are usually unintended and are often found only after wide adoption. One effect can help one group and hurt another at the same time.
- **5.2 Digital Divide.** It covers access, quality, and skill. It is driven by socioeconomic, geographic, and demographic factors, not by individual choice.
- **5.3 Computing Bias.** Bias can enter at every stage of development, intentionally or not, and most often comes in through the training data rather than the code.
- **5.4 Crowdsourcing.** Obtaining input from a large number of people, often online. Citizen science is the standard example. It makes possible things no single organization could fund.
- **5.5 Legal and Ethical Concerns.** Copyright is automatic and the default is all rights reserved. Licenses grant permission in advance. Open source and Creative Commons are license families. Personal data is collected constantly, often in exchange for free services, and aggregating harmless-looking data can identify a person.
